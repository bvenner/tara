## Q1. In Castor 1., what does Castoriadis mean by "instauration"?

"Instauration" is Castoriadis's term for the *active bringing-into-being* of institutions — the collective act of setting-up, establishing, instituting — as distinct from its product (the *instituted*, the established order) and from the passive sense of "institutions" as things found ready-made. The French *instauration* (Latin *instaurare*: to set up, establish, renew) was chosen deliberately to mark institutions as **made, by human activity, and made anew** — the same root family appears in the essay as "instaurated" (the meaning already *instaurated* by society) and "instaurating" (the struggle aimed at *instaurating* real conditions).

In his wider vocabulary this is the instituting/instituted couple: society is not a fixed order but the ongoing self-creation of the *instituting* collectivity, which the *instituted* then hides in heteronomous societies by attributing the institution to an extrasocial source (gods, ancestors, the laws of the market — Castor §1). So "politics = explicit and lucid activity that concerns the **instauration** of desirable institutions" means: the conscious, collective *doing* that brings institutions into being — not administration of what exists, and not a one-time founding. The temporal character matters: Castor §1 immediately adds that this self-institution "is a movement that does not stop" — instauration is ongoing activity, which is why democracy is defined as a *regime* of that activity rather than a set of procedures once installed.

Shorthand: *institution* is the noun of product, *instauration* the noun of the activity, and Castoriadis's definition of democracy puts the weight on the activity. *(Confidence marked per session rule: high for the reading of the essay's own usage — the extracted text was in context; the etymology and the instituting/instituted couple are from general knowledge of Castoriadis's vocabulary, not verified against a source.)*

## Q2. Castor 1. More clearly define "Paragraphenautomat".

A German legal-theory term: literally "paragraph-automaton" — a machine that dispenses statutory paragraphs. *Paragraph* (§) is the numbered section of a code; the *Automat* is the figure of the judge as a device that takes in facts and outputs a verdict by **mechanical subsumption** under the written section, with no discretion, interpretation, or judgment entering the process. The German cousin of Montesquieu's judge as *la bouche de la loi*; the polemical target is classical legal formalism: law as a complete decision procedure, adjudication as mere deduction.

Castoriadis invokes it (§III) as the figure that *cannot exist*: "The judge cannot (and, in any case, should not) ever be a *Paragraphenautomat*" — for two ranked reasons: (a) the lesser, *Rechtslücken* ("holes in the law" — gaps no code can pre-close); (b) the deeper, that every application involves **interpretation** and "at a deeper level, a question of **equity**" — the classical inadequacy (Plato/Aristotle) between the universal form of the law and the singular case, bridged only by the judge's creative work "in the place of the legislator," i.e. recourse to substantive values.

Function in the argument: the Paragraphenautomat is proceduralism's buried premise about who applies the procedures. If the judge is a machine, procedure suffices; since the judge must exercise equity, every legal act is substantive — and procedure "must be applied — by human beings" who must apply it "in its spirit," dragging paideia back in. The term does the same work as *homo juridicus* later in §III: both are fictitious appliances the procedural conception needs and cannot have.

## Q3. Paideia 4.1. You formulated "admitting politics" as a discrete set of options. My initial read: "paideia" could be understood as the meta-meta-process for which Reflect, Explore, Develop, Deploy are moments. (Aside: anticipate work on the relationship between Castoriadis' terms and the meta-systems literature — perhaps a modification of T13.) How are you distinguishing "politics" from "paideia", and what would an alternative goal of "admitting paideia" be?

**The distinction as drawn in Paideia 4.1 — and why it is unstable.** It was pragmatic, as *sites of intervention*: (a) paideia = work on the formation of persons; (b)/(c) politics = work on institutions and explicit power. Castor does not support that as a distinction in kind: each contains the other. Paideia "does not grow up like a plant" but "has to be one of the main objects of a society's political concerns," requiring "substantive political decisions" (§III–IV); and politics is definitionally the instauration of institutions, of which the decisive one *is* paideia ("the Archimedean point here is obviously *paideia*", §II; Athenian procedures were "pieces of a political educational process, of an active paideia", §III). The clean Castoriadian map is **two faces of autonomy**: paideia the *subject-forming* aspect (individual autonomy), politics the *institution-forming* aspect (collective autonomy) — and Castor §II says the two autonomies are "mutually inconceivable." So the menu conflated levels. "Admitting politics" and "admitting paideia" are not alternatives; each dissolves into the other.

**The alternative goal "admitting paideia".** As the goal, it re-ranks everything by one criterion: does this activity form people capable of governing and being governed, questioning institutions, and giving account (*logon didonai*)? The program's assets become curriculum: the cooperative form is a school of self-government (Aristotle's citizen criterion); the **naming policy** (meta-plan §2 — collaborators name the projects) is already a paideic device, unrecognized as one; interviews and action-circle presentations become reciprocal practice in giving and demanding reasons; Reflect becomes the leading meta-process — the QA session itself as its model. Design criteria: (i) the **education dilemma** (§III) becomes a test every artifact must pass — dogmatic teaching makes the program "the political equivalent of a religious ritual"; critical teaching necessarily puts our own institutions in question; (ii) impacts/metrics get a paideic test — do they *develop judgment* or *replace* it; (iii) paideia must be **reciprocal** — "we form them" is the paternalist contradiction; the formative aim applies to the formers first.

Three risks: (a) **the deferral move one level up** — forming subjects in a cooperative niche while society's paideic institutions are untouched is dual-power-of-education; (b) **structural hostility** — Castor §III's privatized public sphere selects against niche-formed subjects (T3's FM8/FM9 measure this); (c) **it cannot be a deliverable** without the philosopher-king error (§IV) — paideia as goal must remain a *movement*.

**On the meta-meta-process reading.** It is right and sharpens the terminal object: if the four meta-processes are *moments* through which participants are formed into self-instituters, paideia is the meta-meta-process structurally — the meta-plan's own doctrine (§4: realizations "dynamically generated" per stage) already describes machinery whose *work* is formation. The alternative placement — paideia as *telos* rather than container — is nearly equivalent, but Castor §IV suggests telos is safer: the common good is conceived by the collectivity each time; a named telos is revisable, a container risks hardening into orthodoxy.

**On the aside.** T13 gains a work package mapping Castoriadis' vocabulary against the meta-systems literature (Explore/Exploit; OODA; PDCA; action-research reflective spiral; double-loop learning), with two questions: whether those cycles are procedural machinery that becomes democratic only when oriented to subject-formation (the "moments of an active paideia" reading), and where "paideia" sits in that literature (its near-absence would be a finding).

## Q4. Paideia 4.2. Not clear what answering this would change in the larger program. There is potentially a map of "worker" and "consumer" cooperatives to "oikos" and "agora". This implies a missing "public" in the cooperative literature — similar to Dewey's critique: a joint worker-consumer process can ignore an affected public. If there are three collectives, a worker-consumer cooperative must continuously orient itself to the "public good" not represented in their transactions — but too far, this paralyzes Develop and Deploy. Restricting the public collectivity to a Reflect process may avoid paralysis-by-analysis. A better answer may depend on T13. Do you agree with the map?

**The map: yes in substance, with one refinement.** The three-collective reading — workers, consumers, a *missing public* — is right, and Dewey is the right citation: the public is constituted by the **indirect** consequences of transactions (*The Public and Its Problems*), so governance built from transaction parties cannot represent it; "eclipse of the public" is the named failure. The refinement: strictly, the worker co-op is not the *oikos* (the private sphere where power "neither can nor should intervene"); it is a voluntary association — *agora* — containing a **miniature ekklesia** (the assembly: explicit, sanction-bearing power over the collective). Mondragon's four-body design is this decomposition (Assembly = ekklesia; Governing + Management Councils = government/administration; Social Council = consultative). Sharper map: worker co-op = agora-association with internal ekklesia; consumer co-op = agora proper; the missing third is not a third kind of cooperative but **the ekklesia of the affected non-participants** — explicit power of the collectivity-as-such, which the movement has nowhere. Corroboration in our corpus: the multistakeholder extension (Imaz's MSCs) approaches the third and misses it — every added class is a transaction party; and Imaz's unwritten rule "the group most affected takes the decision" is a **proto-public device**.

**What answering 4.2 changes.** (i) **Hyphae's three-legged stool is an incomplete agora** — investors, cooperators, technocrats are all transaction parties; the affected public has no leg. The fourth term is not a leg but a different kind of institution. (ii) **It rescues T5 from the Castoriadis condemnation:** impacts become **the sensory apparatus of the affected public** — the medium through which an unrepresented collective tracks indirect consequences — so metrics serve the public's self-clarification instead of replacing it. (iii) "Who is the collectivity concerned" gets answered *per decision class*: transaction parties in the agora; the common good their transactions cannot compute belongs to the public.

**Containment: agreed, with one structural clause.** Restricting the public's voice to Reflect is right — otherwise every Develop/Deploy decision inherits unbounded consultation cost. But Reflect-only-with-no-interrupt is decorative: it repeats the Imaz weakness of governance resting on unwritten rules, and Dewey's publics form *around felt consequences* — late, in bursts. Minimal amendment: public input lives in Reflect, **plus a defined trigger that can pull a decision back into Reflect** (retrospective escalation). Pre-clearance paralyzes; no interrupt marginalizes; interrupt-on-trigger costs little and keeps the ekklesia function real. Meta-plan §5 ("users whose imagination feeds Reflect") was already groping at this.

**T13 dependence: yes — T13 becomes load-bearing.** "Where does the public plug in" is a question about the *coupling* of the meta-processes; the systems literature has the vocabulary: **double-loop learning** is the mechanism by which goals are questioned — a public's claim is a double-loop event; in action-research terms the public enters at reflection-*on*-action, with the trigger converting reflection-in-action to reflection-on-action. T13 grows a third question: which coupling patterns let a non-party collective be heard in Reflect and force a loop-back without becoming a veto over Develop/Deploy.

## Q5. Reformulate answer #1 with a less complex example than Mondragon. Borrowing from category theory (we will need to modify our rules to admit category theory in the Reflect process): a federation of worker cooperatives is like a category of sets, and a single worker cooperative is a set.

Take a federation of two worker cooperatives — a **farm co-op** and a **bakery co-op** — that sell bread to households and share a warehouse.

Each co-op is a **set** — its elements are its members. The **members' assembly is the elements acting as one thing**: a set is nothing over and above its elements deciding together. That assembly *is* the miniature ekklesia living inside the object. (This is why the worker co-op is not the oikos: it is not private life where power stays out; it is a collective that *institutes its own power* over itself.)

The **federation is the category**: co-ops are objects; arrows — farm→warehouse→bakery→households — are transactions (supply, services, sales). The **agora lives in the morphisms**; consumer cooperation is read there too (households meet the federation at its arrows).

Now the point of the map. A household across the street from the warehouse bears truck traffic and wastewater. It is **an element of no object** (not a member) and **a party to no arrow** (buys no bread) — yet the *composition* of arrows (siting the warehouse) hits it. The affected public lives outside the category; no object or morphism represents it; the category's activity still touches it. Dewey's public exactly: constituted by *collateral* consequences, therefore invisible to governance built from objects and arrows. What is missing is not a third kind of cooperative object but a relation the category does not have — a way for *non-elements, non-arrows* affected by composition to bear on **the laws of composition**. Call that the missing ekklesia. "The group most affected takes the decision" (Imaz's unwritten rule) is a folk version of exactly this.

The same shape one level up: Hyphae's three-legged stool is three classes of **object-and-arrow participants** — a richer category, but still a category. The public has no leg; the stool's missing fourth term is not a fourth leg of the same kind.

**Rule change recorded:** the standing convention "category theory / sheaves: long-term only" is amended to admit category theory **in the Reflect process** (objects, morphisms, composition, what lies outside the category). The analogy is admissible here; it stays out of investor-facing material.

## Q7. Paideia 4.4. A potential weakness of Castoriadis' title: it invites thinking of procedure and regime as an either-or. The essay itself does not do this. In my codata project (github.com/bvenner/codata-agda) I have been trying to better understand duality in category theory. Long-winded way of saying: defer 4.4 until further work is done. 4.4 might be a good question for the autonomy-meta-plan.

**Agreed — defer 4.4, and the reason given is part of the answer.** The title poses procedure/regime as either-or; the essay's own formulation is part-whole: "Democratic procedures comprise one — certainly important, but only one — part of a democratic regime" (§III); Athenian procedures are *moments* of the regime's paideia. The essay holds procedure and regime together and condemns only procedure *standing alone* as the definition. The either-or trap in 4.4 (metrics substituting for the good ↔ philosopher-kings defining the good) is the same shape; a duality framing from the codata work — terms mutually determining rather than opposed — may dissolve it: the substantive and procedural as two aspects of one self-instituting activity (§II's "mutually inconceivable" autonomies gestures at this).

4.4 moves into the autonomy-meta-plan as a named open question carrying: the title-vs-text observation (§III quote), the codata lead, and the interim discipline (standing question on every substantive yardstick: *who chose this, and when does the collectivity revise it*) marked **provisional placeholder**, superseded when the duality answer arrives.

As with any procedural realization, these formalizations are necessarily incomplete but could substantially improve realizations of the Develop and Deploy meta-processes. Develop and Deploy meta-plans are in the near future.

**Choke-point concern accepted — #9 amended.** The codata lead becomes one input among several, not a gate: 4.4 proceeds whenever any parallel input matures. Nothing in the program should be blocked on preliminary work — that discipline applies to drafts as much as to codata.

**The new topic: well-formed — T17, codependent with T13.** Division of labor: **T13** is the meaning-and-lineage side (meta-process origins; where paideia and the public fit; the Castoriadis mapping); **T17** is the formalization side — **categorical systems theory** (open systems, structured/decorated cospans, operads, coalgebra) set against **language-based systemology** (general systems theory as conceptual vocabulary — Boulding's hierarchy, the viable-system line), asking what formalization buys and costs relative to structured language. Codependent both ways: T13 without formalization stays at slogans about cycles; T17 without T13 formalizes the wrong objects.

Three properties for T17: (a) **incompleteness doctrine stated up front** — every formalization is a procedural realization and therefore necessarily incomplete, converging with Castor §I's fourth factor (the future "excludes a prior and exhaustive codification" of decisions): formalization cannot close the regime; it can make Develop/Deploy realizations more precise where precision is possible. (b) **The payoff is concrete and near-term**: improved realizations of Develop and Deploy — specification language for the vericoding harness, deployment/digital-twin modeling, and meta-plan §4's "interacting agents exchanging resources over channels" (already systems vocabulary). (c) **Existing formal assets as test cases**: the Plumbing language's formalized multi-agent processes, T15's categorical landscape, the corpus/graph machinery — T17 is not greenfield.
