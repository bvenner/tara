# T3 preliminary findings — what defeated previous attempts to scale cooperative networks?

*Preliminary write-up requested 2026-10-24 (~6 pp). **Scope note (2026-10-25):** the question itself has been challenged — "why didn't cooperative networks scale" inherits a procedural terminal object; see `../democracy-as-regime/` (T16). The findings below stand as failure-mode evidence about the *sub-goal*; their ranking changes under the reframing.*

*Evidence base: the B1 corpus as ingested tonight (12 full texts + 2 record-only works — see `B1-architecture-governance-plan-v3.md` §4.5). Findings are grounded in those documents; citations are `doc key :: locator`. **Status: preliminary** — targeted extraction, not close readings; three paywall-stranded P0 works and the degeneration canon are absent; and the corpus skews to Mondragon, agricultural co-ops, and European cases. Claims below are marked by evidence strength where it matters.*

## 1. The question and why it is the right first question

The meta-plan's end in mind (§1) is a higher-order network of workplace cooperatives that **solves the problems that defeated previous attempts to scale**. That claim is currently unfalsifiable because the problems are unnamed. This note names them, provisionally: a taxonomy of failure modes (FM1–FM9), each with case evidence, followed by a first pass at which modes the Moaiwi/Hyphae/Cooptopia program actually claims to address — and which it does not (§5). The disciplines of the exercise: record failure modes the projects do **not** address; mark every entry's evidence; do not tailor the taxonomy so the program looks like the answer.

A framing finding comes first. The literature does not treat "why cooperative networks fail to scale" as one question but as at least three: why *firms* degenerate or die; why *federations* stay weak; and why the *cooperative economy as a whole* stays small. The failure modes below are sorted accordingly, because a mechanism that kills a single plywood co-op is not the mechanism that keeps a national solidarity economy at 5% of flows.

## 2. Firm-level failure modes

### FM1. Degeneration into a conventional firm

The canonical failure, and the best-evidenced item in this note. The standard mechanisms are separately documented:

- **Dilution through member exit.** When departing members' shares are bought back and replaced by hired non-member workers, membership erodes from inside. The Olympia Veneer Company case is the classic: 125 member-workers in 1921, under 50 working members by 1952, sold to a conventional corporation in 1954 — "the exemplar of the successful plywood worker co-op had 'degenerated' into a capitalist firm" (W2154482800 §III). The process has a tontine structure: each exit concentrates the residue (W2154482800 §III n.15).
- **Mondragon is not exempt.** Non-member workers rose from ~10% of the average co-op's workforce in 1990 to ~70% in 2007; in the industrial group, 29% in 2000, brought down to 12% by 2008 only by deliberate measures (W2154482800 §III). The Slovenian literature names the same triad: **dilution** (owners exit with shares), **concentration** (new workers excluded from ownership), **external buyouts** (W7210828298, "durability" section).
- **Oligarchic capture.** Michels' iron law — an elite within the co-op assumes a commanding role and effectively captures the enterprise (W2154482800 §III).
- **Drift without decision.** Imaz et al.'s Ederlan Tafalla case: bought with intent to convert to a worker co-op, kept as a mixed cooperative by successive financial crises — degeneration by circumstance rather than choice (W4319014659 §10.4).

*Evidence strength: high* — multiple independent cases and a stable mechanism list. *Implication for the program:* none of FM1's mechanisms is an information-technology problem at root; they are membership, capital-account, and succession problems. Any claim that tooling addresses FM1 must be indirect (e.g., making membership accounting cheap enough to maintain).

### FM2. Economic degeneration — the incentive to consume rather than accumulate

Where workers lack individuated claims to capital appreciation, they rationally prioritize current income over long-term capital formation, producing "economically unsustainable" employee-owned businesses (W7210828298, durability section, citing Ellerman; Furubotn & Pejovich). The mirror-image mechanism is the self-financing critique: internally financed co-ops develop a founder "aristocracy," with later members treated as subordinates, and a privileged capital class emerges as workers retire (W2154482800 §III, on Vanek 1977). The degeneration debate is thus not one thesis but two opposed failure stories — *underinvestment* and *over-distribution* — and the capital-account design has to resist both simultaneously.

*Evidence strength: high as theory; the empirical canon (Ward–Domar–Vanek) is not yet in the corpus.* This is exactly the design space the transcripts work out (internal vs. external returns, buy-out dynamics), which makes FM2 the best-grounded target for Hyphae's modeling — and the place where an unchecked theory can masquerade as evidence.

### FM3. Legal-form erosion (companization)

Cooperative law can converge on company law until a co-op "operate[s] like a de facto company": Finnish law permits single-member co-ops, investor shares resembling company shares, extensive departures from one-member-one-vote, and near-unlimited bylaw autonomy over purpose (W2914339720 §4). The honest counterweight in the same source: the cooperative-law doctrine that **voting rights are personal and non-transferable** survives, and voting agreements that hand an outside investor a member's vote are treated as unenforceable (W2914339720 §3.4) — a structural protection that depends on national doctrine, not on the ICA principles (called "somewhat vague, partially outdated"). *Implication:* the legal form is not the protection; instrument terms and governance design must carry it (already recorded in the B1 P0 readings).

### FM4. Conversion pressure from capital markets

When share appreciation becomes visible, successful co-ops convert. New Zealand's largest dairy cooperatives reorganized as investor-owned firms in the late 1980s — "the cooperative form had failed them (or at least their managements) in providing a strategy for survival in international food processing and marketing" (W1539711243, NZ case). The trigger is the owners' entitlement to realize share appreciation (W1539711243). Mondragon's answer — capital admitted as investor-members but **statutorily barred from majority control regardless of investment size** (W4319014659 §10.3.2) — is the counter-design, and Pönkä's Finland is the warning that statutes alone drift (FM3).

## 3. Federation-level failure modes

### FM5. Federations stay weak, or erode

The literature's central pessimism. British producer-co-op federation was weak by construction: the Webbs' "cooperative commonwealth" gave primacy to consumer cooperation and unions, leaving PC support bodies with "limited functions and… far weaker than comparable bodies" (W4389092776, on the Webbs/CWS). Contemporary US analog: the Pro-Coop buying federation with a four-person staff, "limited access to funds for new program development," and county managers who must be individually sold on every idea — "the solution… probably lies in a tighter alliance" (W1539711243, Pro-Coop case). The stranded P0 #3 (W7213299274) attributes post-1990s federation erosion to **economic justification and asset specificity** (record abstract; full text unavailable).

The structural condition on federation strength is stated cleanly in W4389092776: the benefits a member co-op receives must **exceed the financial costs of membership plus the sovereignty surrendered**; different agents perceive different appropriation hazards and different managers different expense preferences, so "the determination of precisely which agents will pay and how much… is apt to be problematical." Strong federations (Mondragon, Legacoop) exist, but the **cautious view** (transaction-cost / principal-agent) says the optimal shelter organization has limited purpose, not maximal scope (W4389092776).

*Evidence strength: moderate-high, and directly relevant.* Hyphae is a federation-coordination technology; FM5 is its principal adversary. Note the asymmetry the corpus hands us: the same sovereignty-cost condition predicts that a federation of *worker* co-ops in an adversarial US environment will face even higher appropriation hazards (legal exposure), which is precisely where the architecture question (Corporation-as-federation) earns its keep.

### FM6. Coordination failure and inability to change

"When cooperative failures have been analyzed, the inability to deal with change is more often than not the cause of failure" (W1539711243). The mechanism set: static transaction inefficiency from democratic voting structures; dynamic weaknesses — opportunism (members/directors are also suppliers), short-sightedness (no share appreciation to anticipate), resistance to change (members' committed production assets), limited scope economies (W1539711243). Manager-incentive and scope-constraint hypotheses both circulate as explanations for coordination deficits versus investor-owned firms (W1539711243). *Caveat:* this study is about **marketing** cooperatives and explicitly excludes worker co-ops (W1539711243, methods) — transfer risk flagged, not resolved.

### FM7. Scale and resource deficits

Worker co-ops innovate with structural handicaps: inability to achieve economies of scale, difficulty accessing knowledge and resources, higher risk aversion (W4400896471, implications section, citing Basterretxea & Martinez; Basterretxea et al.). The empirical answer in the same corpus is **coopetition**: Spanish machine-tool co-ops share R&D units and cooperate with competitors precisely where size/resources bind (W2948895307: "the more vulnerable a firm is in terms of size or limited resources, the more it is willing to collaborate with a competitor" — RP4a, evidenced). The companion review confirms the mechanism's risks: opportunism and knowledge leaks; informal trust is often insufficient and **formal knowledge-protection structures** are needed (W4296129757, on the Galileo project); coopetition suits incremental rather than radical innovation (W4296129757). *Implication:* inter-cooperation is the documented remedy for FM7, but it requires machinery (shared units, protection mechanisms, joint services) — which is a statement of what Hyphae must actually build.

## 4. Economy-level and cultural failure modes

### FM8. Subjective acculturation and belonging loss

The most uncomfortable mode for a technology program. The critical-theory reading adds weight to the degeneration thesis: capitalism shapes "the consciousness, rationality, and depth-psychology of subjects," so cooperatives "will continue to struggle to democratize the economy as they have, thus far, failed to substantially challenge the dominant modes of thought and desire" (W4400976761, abstract + §2). The organizational mirror is Mondragon's regeneration program (RPE): after decades of growth, "a loss of values in its individual cooperatives," with **geographical dispersion eroding the sense of belonging** and hyper-competitiveness — which forced growth in size — "one of the most limiting factors in the application of the formal policies approved" (W4389195852, findings). The regeneration attempts themselves were bounded: the Bagara Herrigintzan association covers one region and "barely twenty cooperatives participate in it"; cooperativization of subsidiaries was limited by geography (W4389195852).

Two cuts follow. (i) **Degeneration is partially reversible** — the existence of a documented regeneration literature (W4389195852's explicit complaint is that prior work focused "almost exclusively" on degeneration) means FM1 is a tendency with a known counter-movement, not a law. (ii) The counter-movement is expensive and slow: it runs on education (Imaz's eight-month ADITU certificate: "we don't have natural born cooperators… we will work so that they become cooperators" — W4319014659 §10.4), unwritten rules, and scaffolding bodies — a **resource cost the plan must budget for, not a design freebie** (B1 P0 readings).

### FM9. Ecosystem failure — hostile or missing institutional environment

Vanek's line: Mondragon's supporting structures exist precisely because of a "hostile environment" (W4389092776). The Slovenian comparison makes the environment variable concrete: employee ownership failed to diffuse due to "insufficient financial incentives… a lack of a clear regulatory framework, administrative barriers… and a lack of awareness," versus the UK/US where tax advantages and clearer frameworks produced scale (W7210828298). Degrowth of the kibbutzim and the Canadian/US conversion stories all sit inside environment shifts, not only internal decay (W2154482800 §III). *Implication:* some of the 5%-ceiling question (Cooptopia) reduces to FM9: capital, tax, and legal infrastructure, not only productive/information technology — a partial challenge to the meta-plan's hypothesis (§3.3) that the limits are "not inherent but technological." The honest statement tonight: **the corpus suggests the ceiling is jointly produced by technology and institution**, and a purely technological Cooptopia would be answering half the question.

## 6. The counter-case (what the pessimists miss)

The corpus contains genuine counter-evidence, which the taxonomy must not bury:

- Worker co-ops show **higher survival rates and more stable jobs in crises** than conventional firms (W4400896471, citing Billiet et al. 2021) — degeneration is a tendency of *successful* co-ops, not a mortality rate.
- The spillover thesis (democratic participation changes subjects and surroundings) is a live counterweight to degeneration (W4400976761, on Pateman 1970) — the same paper's verdict is that degeneration currently has the better of the argument, but the argument is open.
- The Webbs' foundational degeneration evidence is itself "highly questionable" (W4389092776 n.5, on Jones 1975) — the canonical pessimism partly rests on contested history.
- Regeneration is documented and partially successful (W4389195852), and coopetition works where it is tried (W2948895307).

## 7. Gaps, and what would change these findings

1. **The degeneration canon is missing** (Ward 1958; Domar 1966; Vanek 1970/1977; Meade 1972; Cornforth 1995) — WP2's seeds are not yet ingested; FM2's empirical strength is asserted here on secondary citations.
2. **Three stranded P0 works** (#2 W4407091201 federation governance; #3 W7213299274 federation structural change; #5 W4390292511 holding-company tax architecture) — all three bear on FM5/FM9; the federation-level evidence is currently the thinnest part of the taxonomy.
3. **Corpus skew**: Mondragon, agricultural marketing co-ops, and European cases dominate; US worker-co-op federation experience is present mainly via movement sources (gray-lit, not yet ingested). Transfer risk flagged in the screening doc applies to every federation claim above.
4. **No primary failure data.** Everything above is secondhand. The T1 interviews and the sector survey (meta-plan §3.1) can add practitioner accounts of failure; RQ6 (software vs. direct implementation) explicitly requires empirical data.
5. **The scale question is underdetermined.** Nothing in the corpus yet addresses the 5%-of-flows ceiling directly (that is T12); FM7/FM9 suggest the ceiling is institutional + technological jointly, which narrows Cooptopia's hypothesis into something testable.
