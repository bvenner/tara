## 2. Pitch thesis

**One sentence:** A corporation that is a federation of worker cooperatives, whose core product is an integrated modeling capability — financial, planetary, and social — that directs capital top-down to compound returns inside the federation while enabling bottom-up formation of cooperatives with genuine, measurable impact stories.

*(v2) Sharpening — what "raise internal rates of return" means precisely (M3 L415–419):* **internal IRR must exceed external IRR so that external capital can be bought out and replaced by internal capital; external returns are minimized, always.** The end state is a federation capitalized from its own network — "your ultimate source of capital is other organs in the network" (M3 L443).

*(decided 2026-10-22) Three horizons — how the thesis is delivered.* The deck keeps the three stages visible at all times:

- **Short — prototype + interviews.** The stack prototype (built on existing fund-oriented infrastructure) plus impact-fund discovery interviews that define what funds actually need.
- **Medium — a beta product for deployment to funds.** The financial-analysis/back-office wedge with shared back office, books and observability.
- **Long — federation-wide multi-capital modeling and the investment vehicle.** Flow analysis, the hole-plugging thesis, equity-not-bonds, internal/external returns.

One long-horizon question is presented as **open, not decided**: whether the organisation concentrates on **software & systems development** or on **direct implementation**. It cannot be settled by argument — it needs empirical data first.

Expanded:

- **Bottom-up pole.** Worker cooperatives form where their members are, with impact stories that are local and concrete: jobs with dignity, ecological repair, community wealth.
- **Top-down pole.** Capital must be directed quickly and at scale, and internal rates of return must grow — otherwise the federation cannot fund formation fast enough to matter.
- *(v2)* **The mechanism (M1 L127–219).** The modeling capability's first concrete form is **flow and market analysis**: mapping the material and value flows among cooperatives in an area reveals services they already buy from outside firms and demand that exceeds local supply. Formation proposals that fill those holes start with **pre-identified customers, not just market demand** — and the network's preference for buying from its own makes those the highest-confidence businesses in the area. "Plugging holes in the cooperative network is exactly the investment thesis" that attracts capital at above-market expected returns.
- **The mediating institution.** The modeling platform translates between the poles. It evaluates and directs capital against all three domains (financial, planetary, social) at multiple scales without collapsing them into one pre-chosen bottom line. Federation-wide, it compounds: every cooperative's data improves allocation for all. *(decided 2026-10-22)* **Cost accounting is a crucial component**: the platform must be grounded in the existing business processes co-ops actually run (chart of accounts, costing, journals, ERP), and its structural description is stated in **higher-order-network** terms rather than categorical ones.
- *(v2, revised decided 2026-10-22)* **The revenue line — two layers, placed on the horizons (M2 L115–147; M3 L43, 67).** (a) *Medium-term wedge:* a self-serve financial-analysis and back-office stack for impact investing funds — modeling the material outputs and community impact of portfolio companies comparably to their financial-flow models (≈$10K/yr, or cooperative membership with shared maintenance). (b) *Long-term platform:* the **federation-wide** multi-capital modeling capability served to other cooperative networks, aligned capital, and municipalities. Three roles stay distinct: **the funds served**, **the cooperatives funded**, **the fund's investors**. The wedge is a medium-horizon business, not a short-horizon claim.
- *(v2)* **The Corporation is also an incubator (M1 L219, 275).** It writes less capital but gives far more support — warm introductions to network customers, coordination among founders, shared services — because formation, not just funding, is the bottleneck.

## 3. The productive tension (the deck's spine)

Standard models resolve the tension by subordination:

- The conventional holding company gets fast capital and crushes democratic formation.
- The internal-finance cooperative federation (Mondragon's historic path) keeps democratic control and grows capital slowly over decades.

The claim: the tension is not to be resolved but **made productive** — through the modeling capability and through governance innovation. The pitch presents the tension openly and asks the room to help design the resolution. That request is itself the feedback we came for.

## 4. Why a corporation at all (and why a federation)

- The modeling capability has federation-wide network effects, high fixed costs, and needs sustained R&D; a federation-level entity is its natural carrier.
- Speed: a corporate form can absorb and direct equity capital faster than co-op-by-co-op capital formation.
- Federation: member cooperatives keep their formation energy, their impact stories, and — through governance innovation (B1) — structural influence over the Corporation.
- Legal form is **open** (research output, B1), Colorado-first as working base. "A corporation that is also a federation of worker cooperatives" is the design goal, not a settled statute.

## 5. Audience register (Resist & Build DEN-2026 contacts)

Audience: movement-first, with values-aligned capital in the room (network: USSEN, The Democracy Collaborative, Boston Impact Initiative, Kataly Foundation, People's Network for Land and Liberation, Art.coop, The Guild, Income Movement, Democracy @ Work, Next System Studies; Denver-local: Center for Community Wealth, Cooperation 5280).

Design constraints from the register:

- **Capital is subordinate, visibly.** The instrument and governance designs (B1) exist to make that structural rather than promised. State it early.
- **Name the co-optation risk ourselves.** This network's stated worry is that "building alternatives without roots in our resistance risks leading to more exclusion and co-optation." The plan names the risk and shows where the design answers it: governance innovation (B1), aggregation stance (B2), solidarity-fund mechanics (B3).
- *(v2, decided 2026-10-22)* **Language — horizon-dependent register (agreed).** *Short and medium horizons* speak finance and product language — "quantitative finance for cooperative and impact investing," satisficing returns, shared back office — and do **not** lean on the word "solidarity" (M3 L67, 115); *the long horizon* speaks economic democracy and community wealth. Avoid in all horizons: disrupt, exit-as-success, liquidity-event celebration, "human capital", growth-at-all-costs cadence, and any assumed governance template (no a priori one-member-one-vote).
- *(v2)* **Returns doctrine (M3 L315, 427).** An impact investing fund that prioritizes returns to investors is not an impact investing fund — returns recycle to the community, so strong returns mean more people get to invest. The objective structure is **satisficing on capital** (persist, service obligations) while optimizing material community betterment. A credible return thesis is also strategic: it buys **choice of capital** — grants, public banks, philanthropic below-market bonds, mission-aligned investors — where weak rates attract only bad capital. External returns are minimized, always; internal returns are what the network compounds.
- *(v2, decided 2026-10-22)* **"Resilience" stays out of the pitch language.** It is parked as a **long-term deliverable** — a candidate element of marketing plans aimed at different classes of impact investor — not as deck language now. B3's ecological use of the word is unaffected.
- *(decided 2026-10-22)* **Instruments: equity-only for initial investors.** Debt financing is a long-horizon branch only, contingent on improved financial conditions. This settles the posture rather than reconciling the earlier quotes: the deck never has to argue debt-versus-equity, it states the rule.
- **IRR is not hidden.** Rapid IRR growth is stated as the mechanism that funds formation at speed — immediately paired with the question this room is best equipped to answer: what constraints must hold so that compounding capital serves economic democracy?

Anticipated objections → prepared posture (responses offered as open, not defensive):

1. *Why investors at all?* Cooperative capital formation is the historical bottleneck. Aligned **equity** is proposed at speed — initial investors are equity-only; debt is a long-horizon branch — with instrument design (B1) as what makes it acceptable. Feedback sought.
2. *Isn't this a holding company with cooperative branding?* The differentiation is the governance innovation program and the open architecture research — offered for co-design, not asserted.
3. *No fixed aggregation scale = unfalsifiable impact?* No: composition rules are explicit per scale, claims are testable per scale, and the demos make composition choices visible and adjustable. Single-score aggregation is the unfalsifiable option.
4. *Who controls the model?* Open. Model and data governance is part of B1; the default direction is that member cooperatives' data stays theirs.
5. *Won't top-down allocation reproduce extractive dynamics?* Allocation and solidarity-fund mechanics are research outputs (B3) informed by network science and cooperative game theory; democratic checks are B1's subject.
6. *(v2)* *Isn't a returns story parasitic extraction dressed up?* (M3 L303 is the live version of this objection.) Posture: returns-to-the-fund ≠ returns-to-investors (the doctrine above); equity-not-bonds means the fund absorbs failure rather than crushing firms (M3 L159); failed good-faith founders stay in the network as assets (M3 L367).

1. **0:00–0:45 — Why now.** The R&B moment: alternatives need institutions able to hold and direct capital without capture.
2. **0:45–3:00 — Horizon 1 (short) — the problem and the prototype.** Fiduciary single-scalar accounting makes conventional tooling blind to material and community impact; impact funds are small, hyper-local, and priced out of hedge-fund stacks. What exists today: the prototype stack, plus the impact-fund interviews that define what funds actually need. *(Brief live demo — D0 — lands here.)*
3. **3:00–5:00 — Horizon 2 (medium) — the beta product for deployment to funds.** The financial-analysis/back-office wedge (≈$10K/yr or cooperative membership), shared back office, books and observability; the flywheel: stack use as a funding condition, applicant data, gradual privacy deepening — explicitly not a surveillance state.
4. **5:00–6:30 — Horizon 3 (long) — federation-wide modeling and the investment vehicle.** Flow analysis and the hole-plugging thesis; formation with pre-identified customers; incubation ("less capital, more support"); instruments equity-not-bonds, non-voting equity ≈10% (≤20% outside ownership), the barbell, fund-as-bank, the buy-out path (internal > external returns). *(D2 simulator and D3 dashboard land here.)*
5. **6:30–7:30 — What stays research.** Impact without a fixed scale — the aggregation stance (§3, objection 3; *D1 calculator*); governance innovation for the US context; and one open long-horizon question: **software & systems development vs direct implementation**, unresolved pending empirical data.
6. **7:30–8:30 — What we ask of this room.** Instrument feedback, architecture preferences, naming (including the instrument-vocabulary capture), and who wants in.
