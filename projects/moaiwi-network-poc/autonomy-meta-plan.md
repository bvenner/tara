## 1. Terminal object

>  The objective of politics is not happiness but freedom. Effective freedom (I am not discussing here “philosophical” freedom) is what I call autonomy - Castoriadis, Democracy as Procedure and Democracy as Regime, p.5

> Democracy as a regime is therefore the regime that tries to achieve, as far as it possibly can, both individual and collective autonomy and the common good such as it is conceived by the collectivity concerned in each particular case. - Castoriadis, Democracy as Procedure and Democracy as Regime, p.16

> **The project of an autonomous society in the economic domain and beyond it: explicit and lucid self-institution, as far as is possible, of the institutions — economic *and* political — on which collective life depends; individual and collective autonomy; and the common good as the collectivity conceives it in each particular case.** *(Castoriadis, "Democracy as Procedure and Democracy as Regime" §I, §IV — adapted; full text in the corpus, summary in Castor.)*

This replaces — by **re-ranking**, not erasure — the earlier terminal object ("a higher-order network of workplace cooperatives that solves the problems that defeated previous attempts to scale"), which was procedural: it named an organizational *form* and a *procedure* of growth while leaving the substantive goal implicit. The reframing follows Castoriadis' distinction: democracy as *procedure* versus democracy as *regime* — the regime reading being the self-institution of society, with procedures as one part and as moments of paideia. The title's either-or temptation is explicitly refused (QA session 1, Q7): procedure and regime are part-whole and, potentially, dual; the failure mode is procedure *standing alone*.

Consequences stated once and used below: the substantive question ("what is the common good, here, now?") is **kept permanently open and permanently asked** — a recurring act of the collectivity concerned, never a metric, framework, or deliverable. The meta-plan's own discipline survives: this is a plan for planning.

## 3. The three collectives and the missing ekklesia (QA session 1, Q4–Q5)

The map adopted: **workers** and **consumers** correspond to the *oikos/agora* side of economic life — strictly, the worker co-op is an *agora*-association containing a **miniature ekklesia** (the members' assembly: explicit, sanction-bearing power over itself), and consumer cooperation lives in the *morphisms* (the transactions). The third collective — **the affected public** — is missing from the cooperative literature: constituted by the *indirect* consequences of transactions (Dewey, *The Public and Its Problems*), therefore invisible to governance built from transaction parties. The simple case: a warehouse's truck traffic hits a household that is an element of no cooperative and a party to no transaction — the *composition* of arrows touches it, and no institution represents it. "The group most affected takes the decision" (Imaz et al.'s unwritten rule at Mondragon — proto-public device) is the folk version of what is missing: a way for non-elements, non-arrows to bear on **the laws of composition**. *Call that the missing ekklesia.*

Program consequences:

1. **Hyphae's three-legged stool is an incomplete agora.** Investors, cooperators, technocrats are all transaction parties. The missing fourth term is **not a fourth leg of the same kind** but a relation of a different type — the affected public's bearing on the rules of composition. Hyphae's governance design inherits this requirement.
2. **T5 is rescued from the Castoriadis condemnation.** Impact metrics are reconceived as the **sensory apparatus of the affected public**: the medium through which an unrepresented collective tracks indirect consequences. Metrics serve the public's ongoing self-clarification; they never substitute for the political question of the good.
3. **The collectivity-concerned question is answered per decision class:** transaction parties congregate in the agora of transactions; the common good their transactions cannot compute belongs to the public.

**Containment rule (paralysis-by-analysis avoided).** The public's voice lives in the **Reflect** meta-process — not as pre-clearance of Develop/Deploy decisions (unbounded consultation cost) — **plus a defined trigger that can pull a decision back into Reflect** when indirect consequences surface (retrospective escalation). Pre-clearance paralyzes; no interrupt marginalizes; interrupt-on-trigger costs little and keeps the ekklesia function real. Theory base: T13's coupling question (the public's claim as a double-loop event; reflection-on-action).

## 4. Paideia — the other face of autonomy (QA session 1, Q3)

Adopted: "admitting politics" and "admitting paideia" are **not alternatives**. They are the two faces of one activity of self-institution — paideia the *subject-forming* aspect (individual autonomy), politics the *institution-forming* aspect (collective autonomy) — and Castoriadis holds the two autonomies "mutually inconceivable." Paideia is the Archimedean point of participation ("There can be no democratic society without democratic paideia"), and it is itself an object of political decision — so neither face is prior.

**Open (deferred change #2): is paideia the *container* (the meta-meta-process of which Reflect/Explore/Develop/Deploy are moments — Brad's reading) or the *telos* (the good those meta-processes serve)?** Brad's meta-meta-process reading is structurally defensible; the recommendation recorded here is **telos** — because the common good is conceived by the collectivity each time (Castor §IV), a named telos is revisable whereas a container risks hardening into the regime's own orthodoxy. **Decision deferred to T13/T17.**

**Design criteria that fall out now:** the **education dilemma** (Castor §III) is a test every program artifact must pass — dogmatic teaching makes the program "the political equivalent of a religious ritual"; critical teaching necessarily puts our own institutions in question; impacts get a **paideic test** — do they develop judgment or replace it; paideia is **reciprocal** — the formative aim applies to the formers first ("we form them" is the paternalist contradiction).

## 5. Admitting politics — the open question of this plan

The program-activity choice remains open (QA session 1, Q3's original range, now un-bundled from paideia): (a) formation work inside products and cooperatives; (b) **institutional design** — legal forms, the federalism-vs-confederalism question (T16's deferred lead, now promoted); (c) **explicit engagement with public power** — law, policy, the state, the public sphere. The dual-power inheritance made (c) taboo; the reframing reopens it. *This is the decision that determines whether the program stays a business-plan-POC thread or grows a political track of its own* (and therefore whether a new `projects/` entry is warranted — see the placement note in QA session 1, Q6).

Related standing questions: **which collectivity does the conceiving** (partially answered by §3's map — but "who is the collectivity concerned" for any given decision remains a per-decision question); and **Cooptopia's split** (QA session 1, Q3/Q6 provisional content): the 5% ceiling is jointly institutional-technological (T3 FM9 evidence), so the technological half survives as research (T12) while the political half — *why does the institutional order cap the solidarity economy at 5%, and what would change that order* — moves into this plan's political track and stops being research-only.
