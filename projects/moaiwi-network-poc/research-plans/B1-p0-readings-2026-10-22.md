## 1. Imaz, Freundlich & Kanpandegi, "The Governance of Multistakeholder Cooperatives in Mondragon" (2023)

**Object.** The chapter's corrective move is against the familiar reading of Mondragon as "a complex of worker cooperatives." The authors counted **21 multistakeholder cooperatives among the 83 co-ops that vote in the Mondragon Cooperative Congress** — over a quarter, including two of the group's three largest (Eroski, Laboral Kutxa) — and argue the group itself is best understood as a large multistakeholder cooperative (§10.2.4, §10.3.2).

**The federation architecture is the most directly transferable finding.** Individual worker co-ops follow a standard four-body design: General Assembly (all members, highest authority), Governing Council (elected, legally responsible), Management Council (appointed, operational), plus Audit Committee and a consultative Social Council (§10.2.3). Above them, the group is governed by a **Cooperative Congress of 650 representatives from all co-ops, with representation allocated in indirect proportion to firm size so that the largest firms cannot dominate voting**; a Standing Committee elected *from the Area and Division councils* (not directly from Congress) acts as the group's governing council, and it appoints the group's senior manager. The authors call the result an **"inverted conglomerate"**: the General Council has no executive authority over member co-ops, its role is strategic orientation and services, and **ultimate authority rests with the individual co-ops and their worker-members, who can vote to leave at any time** (§10.2.3). Governance runs on "ongoing communication, persuasion, and negotiation, not supervision or executive control from the top."

**Capital is admitted, and structurally barred from control.** Mondragon's "mixed cooperative" legal form admits minority **investor-members**, whose voting weight may be determined by capital contributed — but the Basque Cooperative Law prohibits investor-members from exercising **majority control regardless of investment size** (§10.3.2). Investor-members in practice are legal persons (other co-ops, or the group's own venture fund Mondragon Investments), and only three of 21 MSCs report investment providers in their ownership structure at all (§10.3.2). The **worker-member category is the one constant across all 21 MSCs**, even where workers are not a majority — read by the authors as the institutional residue of "the primacy of labor."

**Power is managed by unwritten rules, not only bylaws.** Eight MSCs have no majority stakeholder in either body, so decisions require agreement among two or three groups; in thirteen, user-members hold a majority (worker-members hold majorities in only four) (§10.3.2). Where the formal structure does not decide, three informal devices carry the load: **unwritten rules** ("the group most affected takes the decision" — in Ederlan Tafalla the investor and parent-co-op representatives simply abstained on a worker-equity increase), **scaffolding bodies** (executive committees, user/collaborator councils created to give a stakeholder its own space), and **cooperative-identity education** (the eight-month ADITU certificate; "we don't have natural born cooperators… we will work so that they become cooperators") (§10.4).

**The failure mode is documented too.** Ederlan Tafalla was bought as a conventional firm with the intention of converting to a standard worker co-op; financial crises have kept it a mixed cooperative ever since — the authors' example of *non-congruent isomorphism*, degeneration by drift rather than by decision; Laboral Kutxa's service role survived only by re-routing around banking regulation (co-ops now 4% of its lending book, offset by a 15-point higher mandatory contribution to group funds) (§10.4).

**What B1 should take.** (i) A working precedent for **capital without control**: non-voting or capped investor classes with a statutory majority bar. (ii) A concrete federation-answer to RQ1/RQ4: authority at the base, a coordinating center with service and strategic functions, and size-weighted-but-inverted representation. (iii) The observation that **governance quality rests on unwritten norms and member education**, which is a resource cost the plan must budget for, not a design freebie. (iv) A caution: the licensing/regulatory environment can hollow out a mechanism silently (Laboral Kutxa).

## 2. Pönkä, "Are Cooperative Societies Transforming into Cooperative Companies?" (2019)

**Object.** A micro-level test of Henrÿ's **"companization"** thesis — cooperative law converging on company law — using the Finnish Cooperatives Act 421/2013, which was explicitly drafted to resemble the Companies Act (§1, §2).

**Four concrete findings of companization** (§4): the Act permits a cooperative with **a single member**; it introduces **investor shares** resembling company shares; it allows **extensive departures from one member, one vote**; and it grants "virtually unlimited bylaw autonomy" over purpose — including bylaws stating that the purpose is to generate profit for shareholders. A Finnish cooperative can therefore "operate like a de facto company."

**What survives.** Pönkä is careful about what is *not* companization: identical board structures are not themselves a symptom, because the general meeting remains supreme; and in cooperative-law doctrine the **right to vote is personal and non-transferable**, so members' voting agreements and arrangements giving an outside investor the right to decide a member's vote are regarded as unenforceable (§3.4). That is a structural protection worth knowing about — and one that depends on national doctrine rather than on the ICA principles, which the author calls "somewhat vague, partially outdated and quite open to interpretation."

**The diagnosis and the honest uncertainty.** Companization is real rather than theoretical, driven by globalization and by capital-market expectations that conflict with the movement's social dimension; the remedy is unclear, and the author explicitly asks whether fighting it is even right, or whether flexibility is a strength (§4).

**What B1 should take.** (i) **The legal form is not the protection.** A cooperative statute can permit near-company behaviour, so the plan's assurance that "democratic control is structural" has to be carried by instrument terms and governance design, not by the word "cooperative" (§1, §4). (ii) **Investor shares are an available device in at least one jurisdiction** — a candidate for WP4's option list, and simultaneously the most-cited entry point of companization; the memo should present it as exactly that tension. (iii) **A research flag for WP2:** the Finnish case is the test of whether US/Colorado cooperative statutes contain comparable flexibilities (single-member co-ops, capital-weighted voting, purpose autonomy), which determines how much protection has to be built into instruments rather than assumed from statute. (iv) Voting-agreement unenforceability is a **candidate mechanism** and a likely US divergence — German-style pooling and voting trusts are enforceable in company law, and the cooperative counter-doctrine does not transfer automatically.

## 3. Joint implications and gaps

- **WP1:** the two papers together supply the comparison matrix's two poles — a federation whose center is deliberately weak (Mondragon's inverted conglomerate) and a legal form whose centre of gravity has drifted toward capital (Finland). The matrix's "co-optation resilience" column can now be populated with mechanisms rather than adjectives.
- **WP4:** investor classes with a statutory majority bar (Mondragon) and investor shares with capital-weighted voting (Finland) are a usable axis: *who may hold capital, and what may that capital vote on*.
- **Gaps to fill from the remaining P0 set:** neither paper covers a federation of *worker* co-ops formed in an adversarial US legal environment; both are single-jurisdiction; and neither addresses the exit/secession question (RQ5) beyond noting that Mondragon co-ops may vote to leave. Carman (1997) and the 2026 federation-structural-change paper are the next reads for that.
