Abstract Through the comparison of conventional firms  (CFs)  with  worker  cooperatives  (WCs),  which are  a  type  of  collective  enterprise  with  significant experience in cooperation, this research aims  to analyse  the  influence  of  external  cooperation  on  the two most prominent factors for the success of enterprises: entrepreneurship and innovation. To this end, a research model is proposed based on contributions from  the  stakeholders'  perspective  of  the  resourcebased  view  of  the  firm  and  tested  with  data  from  a survey  conducted  on  WCs  and  CFs  located  in  two Spanish regions with a long tradition in the cooperative  movement.  The  results  highlight  that  the  influence  of  external  cooperation  on  innovation  performance  is  more  relevant  for  WCs  and  demonstrate that  these  collective  firms  are  not  disadvantaged  in innovation compared to CFs. Furthermore, the results suggest that WCs have a more remarkable ability to compete  and  cooperate  simultaneously,  that  is, to coopete , due to their participative nature.

Plain  English  Summary Cooperation plays an essential  role  in  boosting  both  entrepreneurship  and innovation and workers cooperatives, firms launched by  their  workers,  are  the  best  example  of  this  role. Entrepreneurship and innovation are seen as the two most essential elements to explain business success. Moreover,  competition  is  considered  the  underlying force  that  makes  entrepreneurship  boost  innovation. Nevertheless,  many  studies  show  innovation  only occurs if businesses also cooperate with other stakeholders,  such  as  suppliers,  clients,  and  other  firms and institutions. This research analyses the vital role of cooperation and shows that this role is even more relevant  for  working  cooperatives,  a  type  of  collective  firm  with  a  participative  way  of  making  decisions, than it is for conventional firms, such as liability companies. The principal implication of this study is  therefore  that  the  cooperative  nature  of  working cooperatives can contribute towards the introduction of  innovations  and  towards  a  change  in  social  values  through  their  more  balanced  behaviour  between competition  and  cooperation,  a  strategy  known  as 'coopetition'.

## 1  Introduction

Entrepreneurship  and  innovation  have  been  considered the two most prominent factors for the success of  companies  (Audretsch  &amp;  Thurik,  2001;  Drucker, 1985; Hébert &amp; Link, 1989). On the one hand, entrepreneurial  behaviours,  such  as  risk-taking,  usually positively impact a firm's success since they are necessary  to  compete  in  the  market  (Covin  &amp;  Wales, 2019).  On  the  other  hand,  innovation  performance contributes  towards  maintaining  and  increasing  the company's market position in a competitive and globalised economy (Ferreira et al., 2020).

Another  essential  factor  for  a  firm's  success  is cooperation  with  internal  and  external  stakeholders. The  management  literature  has  paid  notable  attention  to  this  factor  and  has  established  certain  links between  cooperation,  entrepreneurship,  and  innovation,  examples  of  which  include  its  role  in  the  formation of entrepreneurial teams (Harper, 2008), in  managing  the  coordination  of  resources  (Zahra, 2021), and in supporting the development of innovations  (Ozdemir  et  al.,  2023).  However,  according  to certain studies, the emphasis on competition and individualism as the source of a firm's growth has placed cooperation as a complementary factor for entrepreneurship  and  innovation  and,  therefore,  for  a  firm's success  (Bougrain  &amp;  Haudeville,  2002;  Kusa  et  al., 2019).

Notwithstanding, developments of the stakeholder theory applied to the resource-based view (RBV) of the firm attribute cooperation to a more critical role. This  approach  suggests  that  stakeholders'  capabilities  and  competencies  to  cooperate  are  essential  to stimulate  better  firm  performance  (Barney,  2018; Bridoux  &amp;  Stoelhorst,  2022;  Freeman  et  al.,  2021). This  approach  connects  with  the  concept  of coopetition ,  which  means  that  both  cooperation  and  competition  are  the  primary  sources  of  entrepreneurial strategies and innovation and, therefore, a significant element for business success (Brandenburger &amp; Nalebuff,  1996;  Corbo et al., 2023). However, the literature does not explicitly clarify how the links between cooperation, entrepreneurship, and innovation simultaneously take place.

Related  to  this  role  of  cooperation,  a  specific kind  of  enterprise  is  highlighted:  worker  cooperatives (WCs). The theoretical foundation of this kind of collective company comes from the theory of the

labour-managed  firm,  which  studies  how  workers' participation in the ownership, the decision-making process, and the profit distribution affects business performance (Ward, 1958). Worker cooperatives are companies launched by their workers (Bastida et al., 2022;  Guzmán  et  al.,  2020)  and  differ  from  traditional investor-owned firms and conventional firms (CFs)  because  they  put  cooperation  into  practice beyond  the  limits  of  the  firm  itself,  thanks  to  the cooperative  operating  principle  of  inter-organisational cooperation (ICA, 1995). Furthermore, WCs consider the preferences of external stakeholders in their  activities  thanks  to  the  cooperative  principle of  concern  for  the  community  (Cornforth,  2004), which is also related to their capacity for cooperation. Recent studies have even emphasised that WCs are  more  resilient  since  they  have  higher  survival rates  than  other  enterprises  and  generate  higher quality and more stable jobs during economic crises (Billiet et al., 2021).

Nevertheless, several contributions have explained that  WCs have not progressed in some countries, as has been the case in Central and Eastern Europe, due to the explicit support of public authorities in favour of CFs  (Defourny  &amp;  Nyssens,  2021;  Hagedorn, 2014).  Furthermore,  certain  studies  show  that  WCs present some disadvantages compared to CFs. In general,  these  studies  point  out  that  they  are  more  riskaverse  (Dow,  2018)  and  lack  innovation  resources (Novkovic, 2007).

Therefore, regarding the links  among  external cooperation,  entrepreneurship,  and  innovation  and how these links behave in the case of WCs, several gaps in the existing literature lead us to establish two main objectives in this research: to explain the links of  external  cooperation  with  entrepreneurship  and innovation  simultaneously  and  to  examine  whether these links are different for WCs compared to those of CFs.

To reach these objectives, after a first review of the related  literature,  we  develop  a  theoretical  research model  which  is tested by applying partial least squares-structural equation modelling (PLS-SEM) to data from a survey conducted among WCs and CFs. Specifically, we use data from two samples of companies in two Spanish regions that represent international  examples in the field of cooperatives (Basque Country and Andalusia). The samples are composed of 348 WCs and 460 CFs.

This article contributes to the literature in various ways.  Firstly,  this  research  clarifies  how  cooperation  influences  the  two  more  prominent factors that, according to the literature, determine firms' success: entrepreneurship and innovation. Secondly, this article  represents  an  essential  contribution  to  the  literature on WCs because it highlights in this type of company the vital role of cooperation between stakeholders  for  firms'  success  regarding  CFs.  Finally, this  article  helps  to  shed  light  on  the  debate  around whether WCs deserve specific consideration by policymakers and practitioners since they can contribute to a social value change thanks to their more significant ability to compete and cooperate simultaneously.

## 2    Theoretical framework and hypothesis development

## 2.1    Entrepreneurship, innovation, and the role of external cooperation

Entrepreneurship has usually been defined as the ability to deal with uncertainty, the capacity to be alerted to changes in the environment, and the ability to innovate (Hébert &amp; Link, 1989). In this respect, it is considered  a  central  element  for  economic  growth  and development (Acs et al., 2014) and involves the firms putting several entrepreneurial strategies, capabilities, and behaviours into practice which results in a positive  impact  on  firms'  performance  (Covin  &amp;  Wales, 2019).

Moreover,  innovation  has  often  been  defined  in a  broader  sense  as  the  entrepreneurs'  introduction of  'new  combinations'  in  companies  (Schumpeter, 1934).  Innovation  is  also  considered  essential  in explaining firms' performance (Ferreira et al., 2020; Zastempowski, 2023) since it is a driving force that fosters the creation of employment and competitiveness (Audretsch &amp; Thurik, 2001).

Both  entrepreneurship  and  innovation  have  been identified with individual behaviours and competition. Entrepreneurship  is  mainly  related  to  the  identification and exploitation of opportunities by the entrepreneur (Shane &amp; Venkataraman, 2000), and innovation is  generally considered the consequence of the creativity  of  an  individual  entrepreneur  (Harper,  2008). At  an  aggregate  level,  this  emphasis  on  individualistic  behaviours  and  competition has been related to the belief in rationality characterised by maximising self-interest (Harper, 2008; Kusa et al., 2019). In this context, it is problematic for cooperation to be at the core of the analysis and play a more significant role in a firm's success. Nevertheless, implementing cooperative behaviours in any organisation has usually been reiterated each time innovation and entrepreneurship have been mentioned for said success (Ozdemir et al., 2023; Zahra, 2021).

Cooperation implies relations and reciprocity between  people,  by  allowing  individuals  and  social groups  to  have  easier  access  to  information,  facilitating  the  coordination  of  activities,  and  contributing  towards  making  collective  decisions  (Grootaert, 1998). The management literature explains that cooperation  has  a  dual  nature  (Hillebrand  &amp;  Biemans, 2003; Zastempowski, 2023). On the one hand, internal cooperation refers to collaborative behaviour between  stakeholders  inside  the  company,  such  as shareholders,  managers,  and  other  human  resources (Seyhan et al., 2017), while on the other hand, external  cooperation  refers  to  inter-organisational  cooperation  with  stakeholders  external  to  the  company (Ozdemir et al., 2023).

The stakeholder theory provides support to explain the  effects  of  external  cooperation  on  firms'  performance (Freeman, 1984; Freeman et al., 2021). Stakeholders  are  individuals  interested  in  a  firm's  performance, among which customers, suppliers, and other external organisations are found. Recently, this stakeholder theory has highlighted the role of cooperation and shared values among stakeholders in improving performance (Freeman et al., 2021).

In  contrast,  an  influencing  approach  in  the  field of  strategic  management,  the  resource-based  view of the firm (RBV), usually gives specific supremacy to the shareholders in the managerial decision-making process of organisations (Freeman et al., 2021). Managers and entrepreneurs combine rare, inimitable, and non-substitutable resources and capabilities to develop competitive advantages and improve performance (Barney, 1991). However, later contributions of this approach influenced by the stakeholder perspective  (Barney,  2018;  Bridoux  &amp;  Stoelhorst, 2022; Freeman et al., 2021) have suggested the need for a balance between cooperation and competition. Specific  literature  refers  to  this  balance  as coopetition ,  which  occurs  when  entrepreneurial  strategies are built on both cooperation and competition

## 2.2    Hypothesis development step 1: links between cooperation, entrepreneurship, and innovation

If  we  consider  the  stakeholders'  perspective  of  the RBV, cooperation is sufficiently relevant to be at the core of the analysis since it determines both entrepreneurship and innovation. As shown in Fig. 1, we formulate  four  different  hypotheses  showing  these links, which are justified.

In  the  context  of coopetition ,  external  cooperation plays a central role in innovation and entrepreneurship (Corbo et al., 2023). Research shows that external  cooperation  exerts  a  significant  influence on  innovation  since  it  provides  several  advantages to companies, such as sharing and minimising risks and uncertainty, reducing the costs of the innovation process, and obtaining higher levels of performance (Bougrain  &amp;  Haudeville,  2002).  Furthermore,  following  the  RBV,  inter-organisational  cooperation enables  strategic  alliances  between  firms  to  transmit  tacit  knowledge  and  innovations  (Ozdemir  et al.,  2023).  These  contributions  therefore  suggest that  innovation  could  not  occur  without  cooperation, which leads us to formulate the first hypothesis (Fig. 1):

On the other hand, although the literature on entrepreneurship  has  highlighted  various  entrepreneurial behaviours, especially proactiveness, risk-taking, and the propensity to innovate, and the influence of these factors on performance (Covin &amp; Wales, 2019), cooperative  behaviours  have  yet  to  be  included  (Harper, 2008; Kusa et al., 2019). Nevertheless, the stakeholders'  perspective  of  the  RBV  highlights  that  cooperation  and  competition  are  not  mutually  exclusive. In  fact,  entrepreneurial  strategies  are  built  on  both cooperation and competition in a process of coopetition (Brandenburger &amp; Nalebuff, 1996; Czakon et al., 2020), as H2 shows in Fig. 1.

Moreover,  the  literature  has  established  a  robust link between entrepreneurship and innovation (Audretsch  &amp;  Thurik,  2001;  Covin  &amp;  Wales,  2019; Drucker, 1985; Ferreira et al., 2020). Specific studies consider  innovation  performance,  that  is,  the  output of  the  innovation  process,  as  a  measure  of  a  firm's performance  determined  by  proactiveness,  risk-taking, and the propensity to innovate (Arzubiaga et al., 2018). This is shown by H3 in Fig. 1.

Lastly,  according  to  the  stakeholders'  perspective of  the  RBV,  entrepreneurs  usually  harmonise  external cooperation to put entrepreneurial strategies into practice, which  improves  the firm's  performance (Barney, 2018; Freeman et al., 2021). External cooperation, therefore, can encourage proactiveness (Kusa et  al.,  2019),  it  can  be  a  determinant  of  creativity

(Zastempowski, 2023), and it can reduce risk aversion (Ozdemir  et  al.,  2023).  Entrepreneurial  behaviours mediate between external cooperation and the output of  the  innovation  process,  that  is,  innovation  performance. This is what H4 shows in Fig. 1.

Summarising,  the  following  three  hypotheses  are formulated (Fig. 1):

- H2:  External  cooperation  positively  and  directly influences a firm's entrepreneurial behaviours. H3:  Entrepreneurial behaviours positively  and directly influence a firm's innovation performance. H4: External cooperation positively and indirectly influences  a  firm's  innovation  performance  by
- mediating entrepreneurial behaviours.

## 2.3    Hypothesis development step 2: comparison between working cooperatives and conventional firms

The  study  of  links  between  external  cooperation, entrepreneurship,  and  innovation  is  fascinating  in the case of collective firms and, more specifically, in the case of WCs. As a kind of labour-managed firm (Ward, 1958), one essential characteristic of WCs is that most of the ownership is in the hands of workers and, therefore, collectively managed (Díaz-Foncea &amp; Marcuello,  2015).  WCs  are  generally  more  resilient to economic crises thanks to their greater capacity to adapt to environmental changes (Billiet et al., 2021). The  primary  source  of  this  advantage  is  that  these enterprises are based on the values and principles of the  cooperative  movement,  such  as  inter-organisational  or  external  cooperation,  democratic  management of their members, and concern for the community (ICA, 1995). Therefore, they are companies with a cooperative nature.

Nevertheless, certain contributions have explained that WCs have not thrived in several countries, as has been the case in Central and Eastern Europe, due to the explicit support of public authorities in favour of CFs (Defourny &amp; Nyssens, 2021; Hagedorn, 2014). Furthermore, various studies point out that the different cooperative nature of WCs regarding CFs not only conditions how WCs act (Díaz-Foncea &amp; Marcuello, 2015)  but  can  also  be  an  obstacle  to  their  survival (Errasti et al., 2017). More specifically, other studies find that WCs present weaknesses compared to CFs, such as high-risk aversion (Dow, 2018) and a lack of innovation resources (Novkovic, 2007).

However,  these  results  are  inconclusive  according  to  other  studies  (Monteiro  &amp;  Straume,  2018). For instance, in the specific case of innovation, Basterretxea  and  Martinez  (2012)  found  no  differences between  WCs  and  CFs  regarding  innovation  capacity. Moreover, the study of Basterretxea et al. (2019) also concluded that R&amp;D cooperation between WCs is a specific strategy for success while they carry out innovative projects.

It is therefore possible to complete the hypothesis development  suggested  in  Fig.  1,  by  comparing  the links  between  external  cooperation,  entrepreneurial behaviours, and innovation performance in WCs and CFs. In Fig. 2, two new moderating links are established in which we hypothesise that the direct effect of external cooperation on innovations is greater for WCs  (H5)  and  that  the  mediating  role  of  entrepreneurial  behaviour  between  external  cooperation  and innovation  performance  is  greater  for  WCs  (H6). There  are  several  arguments  that  support  these  new hypotheses.

On the  one  hand,  cooperation  in  WCs  can  better stimulate  innovation  performance  than  CFs  because they  base  their  modus  operandi  on  bonding  ties  of trust  and  loyalty  among  the  different  stakeholders, and  therefore,  they  have  more  significant  incentives and advantages for cooperation than CFs (Cornforth, 2004; Thompson, 2015). In addition, the concern for the community of WCs, according to the principles of the cooperative movement (ICA, 1995), is an incentive  to  carry  out  cooperation  agreements  with  other actors in the field of innovation. Therefore, the fifth hypothesis is the following (Fig. 2):

H5: The direct influence of cooperation on innovation performance is greater in WCs than in CFs.

From  this  perspective, external cooperation is an essential asset that the team of  workers  of  this  kind  of  firm  mobilises  through proactive, innovative, and risky strategies due to the bonding ties of trust and commitment they share. ## 3    Methodology

## 3.1    Data

In order to test the research hypotheses, we surveyed WCs and CFs in the Spanish regions of the Basque Country  and  Andalusia.  These  two  regions  were selected  because  they  show  the  common  feature  of having  a  long-standing  tradition  in  the  cooperative movement, although they have different levels of economic development according to the GDP per capita, the  unemployment  rate,  and  the  risk-of-poverty  rate (Table  1). The final sample was obtained using a proportional  stratified  random  technique  with  an  error level  of  6.5%  and  a  95%  confidence  level. - Propensity to innovate  (PropINN) :  When  the dependent variable is innovation performance, the dimension of innovativeness of the EB construct is measured with the indicator propensity to innovate since it is an input of the innovative process. In this case, the formative indicator propensity to innovate (PropINN) is a continuous variable that varies  between  '0'  and  '100',  representing  the percentage  of  the  budget  of  the  company  dedicated to innovative activities (not just R&amp;D).

The  first is  annual  formal  planning  activities  to  anticipate the  future  (value  1  or  otherwise  0).  The  second one  is  remaining  current  regarding  any  market changes  (value  1  or  otherwise  0).  The  third  one is  the  search  for  and  exploitation  of  new  market opportunities (value 1 or otherwise 0). Finally, the fourth one is fostering the training and qualifications of the firm's employees (value 1 or otherwise 0). This proactiveness variable corresponds to the aggregation of the four types of behaviours.

## 3.3    Data analysis

Given  the  hypothesised  relationships  between  the latent variables, ExtCOOP, EB, and INNP, we applied  structural  equation  modelling  (SEM)  as  a multivariate  statistical  analysis  method,  particularly partial least squares (PLS-SEM). SmartPLS 4.0 software developed by Ringle et al. (2022) has been used in this research to report the results of the PLS-SEM analysis.

There  are  several  reasons  to  use  PLS-SEM  (Hair et al., 2022): (1) The focus of the study is as explanatory as it is predictive of the primary dependent variable;  (2)  as  happens  in  this  study,  different  types  of scales are supported in PLS-SEM (continuous, ordinal,  interval,  ratio  scales,  and  even  binary  items); (3) the sample is not very large, although PLS-SEM works very well with both small and large samples; (4)  the  research  model  is  complex  since  it  includes direct, mediating, and moderating relationships.

## 4    Results

## 4.1    Assessment of global model fit

The first step of a PLS analysis is assessing the global model fit  (Henseler  et  al.,  2016).  Thus,  three  different tests of model fit based on the bootstrap were estimated:  the  standardised  root  mean  squared  residual (SRMR),  the  unweighted  least  squares  discrepancy (dULS),  and  the  geodesic  discrepancy  (dG).  If  any of  these  tests  exceed  bootstrap-based  95%  (HI95) and  99%  percentiles  (HI99),  it  is  unlikely  that  the model is true. In the case of this study, the three tests of model fit for the estimated model of the full sample are under the HI95 and HI99 percentiles. Similar results were obtained for models of the two subsamples (WCs and CFs). This means that the three models cannot be rejected. Furthermore, it was necessary that  the  threshold  of  0.08  in  the  SRMR  value  could not be exceeded. This happens with values of 0.016 for the full-sample model, 0.029 for the WC sample model, and 0.021 for the CF sample model (Table 4).

## 4.3    Assessment of the structural model

Table 7 shows the different tests to assess the structural  model  of  Fig.  2.  The  first  important  step  is  to examine whether all VIFs in the full-sample and the two subsample models are lower than 3.3. As Table 7 shows, no multicollinearity problem exists.

The second step is the assessment of the explained variance ( R 2 ) of the endogenous variables. The coefficient of determination ( R 2 ) of a variable has to exceed the  minimum  value  of  0.1  to  be  considered  acceptable for the predictive capacity of the model for that variable. This condition is met for innovation performance  (INNP)  and  entrepreneurial  behaviours  (EB) in the three models. In the case of the R 2 for innovation  performance  (INNP)  variable,  this  ranges  from 0.249 in the case of the CF subsample model to 0.260 in the case of the WC sample model. An R 2 value of 0.2  is  relatively  high  and  acceptable  by  behavioural research standards (Hair et al., 2022).

## 4.4    Multigroup analysis

Finally,  a  multigroup  analysis  is  conducted  to  test H5 and H6, which refer to the comparison between the  WC  sample  model  and  the  CF  sample  model. This  allows  us  to  test  whether  pre-defined  data groups  significantly  differ  in  their  group-specific parameter estimates (e.g. outer weights, outer loadings, and path coefficients). SmartPLS 4.0 provides outcomes  that  are  based  on  bootstrapping  results from every group.

However, before comparing path coefficients across both groups, it is necessary to ensure the measurement invariance of constructs so that it is possible to ensure that the effect of being either a WC or CF is  restricted  to  the  path  coefficients  of  the  structural model and not to the parameters of the outer model. To this end, a MICOM, a procedure with three stages, is  carried  out  (Henseler  et  al.,  2016).  Reaching  at least the second step (partial measurement invariance) is necessary to compare and test potential differences between path coefficients  and  other  parameters  generated in each data group. As Table 8 illustrates, the MICOM procedure reaches the second step, and consequently,  it  is  possible  to  continue  with  the  multigroup analysis.

Table  9  shows  permutation  test  results.  According to these results, H5, concerning the link between external cooperation (ExtCOOP) and innovation performance  (INNP),  is  not  supported since  the  differences between the path coefficients of the two models are not significant. However, H6, concerning the indirect effect through the mediation of entrepreneurial  behaviours  (EB),  is  supported since  differences between the values of the effects between both models are significant at a level of 99.0%, applying 5000 permutations and with p -values based on a one-sided test. Therefore, the partial mediation effect is stronger for WCs.

## 5    Discussion and contribution

Economic and management literature generally considers innovation and  entrepreneurship  crucial to firms'  performance  (Drucker,  1985;  Ferreira  et  al., 2020; Covin &amp; Wales, 2019). On the contrary, cooperation  habitually  appears  in  many  studies  as  complementary  to  innovation  and  entrepreneurship  and, therefore,  not  at  the  core  of  the  analysis  (Bougrain &amp;  Haudeville,  2002).  Deep  down,  what  is  underlying  here  is  that  entrepreneurship  and  innovation  are considered individualistic phenomenon (Harper, 2008;  Kusa  et  al.,  2019).  However,  if  this  were  the case, entrepreneurship and innovation could not occur and neither could firms' success and economic development.

Specifically,  this  research  has  shown  that  external  cooperation  with  other  stakeholders  plays  a relevant role by directly influencing proactive, creative,  and  risk-taking  business  strategies  (H2)  and innovation  performance  (H1).  Furthermore,  these business strategies exert a direct influence on innovation  performance  (H3)  and  behave  as  mediators between  external  cooperation  and  innovation  performance (H4). The main argument is that the role of the entrepreneur is to develop cooperation as one of the objectives to increase the competitive advantage  and  improve  innovation  performance,  such  as the new stakeholders' perspective of the RBV supports  (Barney,  2018;  Bridoux  &amp;  Stoelhorst,  2022; Freeman et al., 2021). In this way, this research is in  line  with  those  studies  that  claim  that  cooperation  is  an  essential  source  of  external  relations  for entrepreneurial strategies (Kusa et al., 2019). Consequently,  the  result  of  this  study  supports  those contributions  which  highlight  that  entrepreneurial strategies  should  be  built  not  only  on  competition but also on cooperation in a process of coopetition to  improve innovation performance and attain success for the firm (Brandenburger &amp; Nalebuff, 1996; Corbo et al., 2023).

In  this  context,  WCs,  a  type  of  labour-managed firm  which  follows  the  cooperative  principles  of inter-organisational cooperation and concern for the  community,  represent  an  excellent  example  of the  relevant  role  that  external  cooperation  plays  in

entrepreneurship  and  innovation.  Specifically,  the results  of  the  analysis  have  firstly  shown  that  there are  no  differences  between  WCs  and  CFs  in  the direct  relation  between  external  cooperation  and innovation  performance  (H5),  which  supports  the findings of other studies that point out that WCs do not  have  a  lower  innovation  capacity  compared  to CFs (Basterretxea &amp; Martinez, 2012). Furthermore, the results also show that the role of external cooperation  as  an  antecedent  of  entrepreneurial  behaviours  and  innovation  performance  is  stronger  for WCs than CFs (H6).

These  results  are  mainly  related  to  the  greater influence in WCs of external cooperation on entrepreneurial behaviour. The bonding ties of trust and loyalty  among  the  team  of  workers  who  manage WCs  create  significant  incentives  and  advantages not  only  for  the  internal  organisation  but  also  for inter-organisational  cooperation,  thereby  stimulating entrepreneurial behaviours which boost innovation  performance  more  strongly  (Cornforth,  2004; Thompson, 2015; Guzmán et al., 2020). While competing in the markets, the team of workers of these companies cooperate with each other and with other stakeholders  to  develop  risky,  proactive,  and  creative strategies which lead to innovation and the success  of  the  firm  (Basterretxea  &amp;  Martinez,  2012; Errasti et al., 2017). In this way, these results reinforce  the  assertion  that  entrepreneurial  strategies and  innovation  are  the  consequence  of  a  process of  coopetition  (Brandenburger  &amp;  Nalebuff,  1996; Corbo et al., 2023). However, while if coopetition is an  intentional  strategy  of  CFs  for  business  success (Czakon et al., 2020), it is for WCs the consequence of their specific nature based on the values and principles  of  the  cooperative  movements  (Díaz-Foncea &amp; Marcuello, 2015).

Summarising,  this  research  contributes  to  the  literature  in  different  ways.  Firstly,  it  clarifies  how cooperation  influences  the  two  more  prominent  factors that, according to the literature, determine firms' success: entrepreneurship and innovation, putting cooperation at the core of the analysis. Secondly, this article represents an essential contribution to the literature  on  WCs.  It  highlights  the  important  role  of cooperation  between  stakeholders  for  firms'  success in  this  type  of  company  by  developing risky, proactive, and innovative behaviours and showing that they are not disadvantaged compared to CFs in innovation

capacity. Finally, this article suggests that  WCs deserve specific consideration since they can contribute to a change in social values thanks to their more significant ability to compete and cooperate simultaneously based on their different  and  specific  nature. According to the organisational citizenship behaviour (OCB) theory, cooperation arises in these collective firms from the perception of social justice held by the firm's members (Organ, 2018).

## 6    Implications

Regarding the implications of these results for practitioners,  a  positive  attitude  to  cooperation  could  be critical in any firm (Xie et al., 2023). In the specific case of WCs, reinforcing cooperation can contribute to  overcoming  specific  problems  that  WCs  present when they have to innovate, such as the inability to achieve economies of scale or the difficulty of accessing knowledge and particular resources (Basterretxea &amp; Martinez, 2012; Basterretxea et al., 2019; Novkovic, 2007). At the same time, promoting cooperation with external stakeholders contributes to reducing the higher risk aversion of WCs, facilitating the innovative strategies of these firms to achieve higher innovation performance (Basterretxea et al., 2019). Furthermore, when some WCs have moved away from their essence, they have gone through a process of 'degeneration'  (dilution  of  cooperative  values  and  principles)  that  has  even  led  them  to  bankruptcy  (Errasti et al., 2017).

Concerning  implications for policymakers, the results of the research call for them to be more interested in promoting measures related to cooperation in any  firm,  as  they  would  have  a  consequence  on  the innovative  capacity  of  their  economies.  These  policies could be more efficient than the habitual policies of  subsidising  the  formation  of  start-ups  with  low possibilities  of  success.  In  general,  the  latter  have been failing (Shane, 2009).

In  the  specific  case  of  WCs,  policymakers  may also be interested in promoting the creation of these collective companies more strongly than they are currently, as their nature is linked with cooperation and, consequently,  with  entrepreneurial  behaviours  and innovation performance. Moreover, the greater cooperative  experience  of  WCs  could  contribute  to  the diffusion of innovations. On the other hand, because WCs based their activities on the commitment to the environment, that is, they consider the general interests of stakeholders (Cornforth, 2004), their cooperative  essence,  entrepreneurial  behaviours,  and  innovation  performance  are  expected  to  have  positive external effects on society, such as greater job stability and greater quality employment, contributing to a social value change.

## 7    Conclusion and limitations

The  main  objective  of  this  study  has  been  to  place the role of cooperation in a central position in firms' competitive advantage. Specifically, this research has contributed to disentangling the relationship of cooperation with innovation and entrepreneurship, distinguishing between two types of firms: WC and CF.

From the results of the empirical analysis carried out,  the  following  conclusions  can  be  established. Firstly,  external  cooperation  is  at  least  as  influential for  companies  as  innovation  and  entrepreneurship since it determines innovation performance and entrepreneurial behaviours in any firm. Secondly, the influence  of  cooperation  on  entrepreneurial  behaviours and innovation performance is even more critical for WCs,  a  type  of  collective  firm,  than  for  CFs.  This last  idea  confirms  that  WCs  have  a  more  collective nature  than  CFs  and  benefit  from  this  characteristic to overcome the different obstacles they must face in the development of their activity. Therefore, WCs are a  more  sustainable  entrepreneurship  model  (Bastida et al., 2022).

Lastly, this study is not free of limitations. Although  the  statistical  method  is  completely  valid and the  sample  size  is  appropriate  for  this  research, it  would  be  interesting  to  use  different  measures  to value  the  same  aspects  and  also  to  apply  the  same study to another sample in other territories of Europe where cooperatives are not so extended. This would reinforce  the  conclusions  and  expand  them  to  other contexts. Moreover, according to the findings of this study,  it  would  be  interesting  to  analyse  in  future research how to enhance cooperation between stakeholders through public policies, especially in the case of WCs, in the context treated herein.
